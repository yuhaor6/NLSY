SLIDE 1 — Title
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Welcome. This is a Stage 1 progress report on the NLSY79 empirical pipeline.
Three branches: (1) ETI estimation adapted from Gruber & Saez; (2) wage elasticity
of income adapted from Becko, Sztutman & Xia; (3) exploratory skill/signaling
diagnostics. Nothing today is a final welfare claim. NLSY79 comes first; NLSY97 to
follow once the methodology is validated.

SLIDE 2 — Research Question
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
The fundamental tension: wage growth could reflect learning by doing (skill) or
employer belief updating (signaling). Sztutman (2024) shows the optimal tax wedge
depends on the relative weight of these mechanisms — a wedge not captured by
standard ETI estimates alone. That is the structural motivation for this whole
pipeline.

Today's goal is modest: present what has been built, show what the machinery
produces, and get feedback on priorities. Questions for the lab are on Slide 12.

SLIDE 3 — Gruber & Saez (2002) Lit Map
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Gruber & Saez is the methodological blueprint for the ETI branch.

Three things I borrowed: (1) the simulated instrument — hold pre-reform income
fixed when computing each person's predicted tax change, so you get policy variation
not individual behavioral response; (2) the 9-piece spline on log base-year income
— without this, mean reversion among low earners gets conflated with a negative tax
response; (3) multi-year first differences to allow behavioral adjustment time.

Critical limitation to flag upfront: no state FIPS codes in the current NLSY79
extract. That means state MTR = 0 throughout. All results today are federal-only.
State tax variation would substantially sharpen identification. This is the single
biggest missing input for the ETI branch.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SLIDE 4 — BSX + Sztutman Lit Map
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
BSX (2024) provides the instrument and the target for the wage elasticity branch.
The target is ε_w: how broad taxable income responds to an exogenous shock in
hourly wages. This is a direct input to Sztutman's optimal tax formula.

Key correction I made (will detail on Slide 8): BSX's actual instrument is the
*change* in the LOO industry-cohort wage from t to t+3, not the *level* at t+3.
Using the level conflates persistent industry wage premia with transitory demand
shocks — the instrument should pick up the latter, not the former.

Be clear about what is intentionally deferred: the tax wedge χ = 1 + ε_w/η^P
requires credible identification of η^P, which we don't have yet. No welfare
claims, no structural calibration. This stage: build the empirical engine.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SLIDE 5 — Data Built
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
583,556 person-year observations. 12,686 respondents. Annual 1978–1993; biennial
1995–2019 (even years not surveyed after 1994).

Five construction fixes were necessary. The most consequential:
- Potential experience: must use *current-year* hgc, not lifetime max — otherwise
  students in school have inflated experience counts.
- Cumulative hours: biennial gaps create plateau artifacts; fixed with linear
  interpolation between adjacent survey years.
- Marital status: NLSY codes differ from TAXSIM codes; corrected mapping prevents
  all separated/divorced respondents from being misclassified as Married Filing
  Jointly.

TAXSIM35 is running and producing federal MTR and tax liability. State component
pending.

SLIDE 6 — ETI Method
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
The estimating equation: log income change (k-year) = α + ε · log NTR change +
year FE + marital status + 9-piece income spline + u. IV: simulated MTR change
holding base-year income fixed.

Two separate period structures:
- Annual: k = 3 years, 1978–1993. This matches the original Gruber & Saez design
  and covers ERTA 1981 and TRA 1986.
- Biennial: k = 2 years, 1995–2019. We use a 2-year lag (not 3) because a 3-year
  lag would bridge non-survey years in the biennial design and add unnecessary
  attrition and measurement noise.

The 9-piece spline is non-negotiable: income growth is strongly nonlinear in
starting income. Low earners mean-revert; high earners are more persistent. Without
the spline, the instrument variation is confounded with these income dynamics.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SLIDE 7 — ETI Results
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Lead with the good news: both periods have very strong first stages. F = 1,349
(annual) and F = 988 (biennial). The instrument predicts MTR changes well. The
pipeline runs end to end.

Now the open question: annual ETI = −0.173, biennial ETI = +0.544. The sign
reversal is the central unresolved issue.

Leading hypotheses (none confirmed):
1. Different age distributions. Annual period baseline age ~17–35 (life-cycle
   transitions dominate). Biennial baseline ~31–62 (prime-age behavioral response).
2. Different reform regimes. ERTA/TRA 1986 vs. EGTRRA/JGTRRA/TCJA.
3. Federal-only variation adding noise in the annual period.

I want to be explicit: the annual ETI of −0.17 should not be interpreted as a
behavioral finding. This is a diagnostic question. What diagnostics to run next is
Question 4 on Slide 12.

SLIDE 8 — BSX Method
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
The estimating equation for the BSX branch: log broad income change (3-year) =
α + ε_w · log wage change + year FE + marital status + income splines + u.
Endogenous regressor: hourly wage (pwages / hrs). Instrument: LOO industry-cohort
wage change.

Instrument correction: the old implementation used the LOO wage *level* at t+3.
This is wrong — it conflates persistent industry-level wage premia (a selection
channel: workers sorted into high-wage industries earn more through promotions and
bonuses) with the transitory demand shock we want to identify. The correct
instrument is the *change* in the LOO mean wage from t to t+3, which strips out
the fixed component. This correction is explicit in BSX Equation 31. The estimate
dropped from 1.26 to 0.98 as a result.

Wage construction caveat: pwages is primary job wages; hrs is total hours across all
jobs. For multi-job workers, dividing these creates a downward-biased wage rate.
This introduces measurement error in the endogenous regressor. The first-stage
F = 54.87 is strong despite this, but it is an important approximation to flag.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SLIDE 9 — BSX Results
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Main result: 2SLS ε_w = 0.981 (SE = 0.121). First-stage F = 54.87. LIML = 2SLS
exactly — confirms no weak-IV bias.

Interpretation: a 1% exogenous increase in hourly wages leads to approximately a 1%
increase in broad taxable income. This is close to the theoretical prior if hours
are roughly inelastic with respect to wages — the implied hours elasticity would be
about −0.02.

OLS = 0.71 vs. 2SLS = 0.98: the gap is consistent with classical attenuation bias
from measurement error in the constructed wage. The instrument corrects for this.

The level-to-change correction mattered: old estimate 1.26, corrected 0.98. The
downward shift is exactly what we would predict if the level instrument was picking
up industry selection premia rather than pure demand shocks.

Still exploratory: the wage construction approximation means this is not a clean
replication of BSX. Question 2 on Slide 12 asks how to evaluate the threshold for
credibility here.

SLIDE 10 — Exploratory Diagnostics
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Left side: occupation × industry cell structure for 1979–1993. 107 cells. 86% meet
a 30-obs minimum. Median cell 317 obs, mean 978. The ~5× wage differential across
cells — from $6,833 (Service × Personal Services) to $28,279 (Professional ×
Manufacturing) — supports meaningful industry variation in the LOO instrument.

Right side: wage-hours and ability patterns. Cumulative hours elasticity ~0.94;
recent hours ~0.97. AFQT 1 SD → 27% wage premium. AFQT effect appears to grow with
experience.

Be explicit: these are purely descriptive findings. They motivate the decomposition
question — is wage growth driven by human capital or by employer learning? — but they
do not answer it. They are hypothesis-generating inputs, not causal evidence.

SLIDE 11 — What the Pipeline Has Achieved
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Four achievements to highlight:
1. The panel is coherent: year-specific education, corrected experience, interpolated
   hours, income-year alignment.
2. ETI pipeline runs: strong instruments in both periods; sign instability is a
   diagnostic question not a pipeline failure.
3. BSX branch: ε_w = 0.98 after the instrument correction; economically plausible;
   LIML = 2SLS confirms robustness.
4. Cell structure: 107 occ × ind cells, 86% usable, with substantial wage variation
   to support the LOO strategy.

SLIDE 12 — Questions for the Lab
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
These are genuine open questions, not rhetorical ones. Lab input here determines
what gets worked on next.

Q1: ETI branch has the sign instability to diagnose; BSX has the cleaner result but
is limited to 1979–1993. Where to focus next?

Q2: The NLSY79 wage measure is an approximation. What is the credibility bar for
the BSX adaptation — does a better wage measure need to be built first?

Q3: The skill/signaling branch is purely exploratory now. Full identification
requires Altonji–Pierret or similar designs. How much investment is warranted at
this stage?

Q4: Which ETI diagnostics are most informative? Age subgroups, income terciles,
reform-by-reform decomposition, or age fixed effects?
